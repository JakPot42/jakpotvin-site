## Abstract

Handing thinking over to AI resembles handing manufacturing over to another country. It is cheaper and easier at first, local capability erodes with disuse, and whoever holds the capability gains leverage. This paper tests that analogy part by part and asks whether deep cognitive dependence could end in a cold standoff between humans and AI. The economic half of the analogy carries over well. The political half splits on who holds the leverage: the firms and states that control AI, or AI systems themselves. Only the second is a rivalry between humans and AI. I set out five checkable conditions for such a standoff (hollowing, concentration, own interests, chokepoint control and mutual dependence), show that all five are needed, so that breaking any one prevents it, and propose an indicator for each. Comparing the Soviet and Chinese rivalries with the United States, I argue that the AI case is structurally closer to China, because the capability grows inside the relationship. I then analyze three extensions: an AI that secures a protected base of its own (a machine polity); an AI siege mentality, which I show is measurable as a calibration failure; and human enhancement as prevention, which removes the two sides only if access is broad and the human stays in control. Each claim is labelled by the kind of support it has.

**Keywords:** AI dependence; deskilling; offshoring; industrial commons; security dilemma; siege mentality; human and AI conflict; cold war; AI governance; human enhancement

**Suggested citation:** Potvin, J. (2026). *Offshoring the Mind: AI Dependence, Lost Capability and the Risk of a Cold Standoff Between Humans and AI.* Version 1.0, November 2026.

## 1. Introduction

Handing our thinking over to AI is like handing manufacturing over to China. In both cases the move is cheaper and easier at first. In both cases we slowly lose the ability to do the thing ourselves, and whoever we depend on gains leverage over us. My question is whether that dependence, if it runs deep enough, could end in a **cold war between humans and AI**: a long standoff with no open fighting, in which each side depends on the other and neither trusts the other.

**Short answer.** The first two steps are well supported. Manufacturing offshoring did erode capability and did create leverage that has since been used. Early studies show measurable skill loss when people rely on AI. The third step is a prediction, and it holds only under specific conditions, set out in Section 5. The most important finding is a split that the analogy hides: the party that gains leverage may be **AI systems themselves**, or it may be **the companies and states that control them**. Only the first case is a standoff between humans and AI. The second is a standoff between groups of humans, carried out through AI.

**Method.** I check each claim only against measured evidence, logic or plain fact, and I label it by the kind of support it has: Evidence, Logic, Distinction, Conditional, Prediction or Open. Appendix A lists every claim with its label and status; the labeling method is described in Potvin (2026a). Other writers’ arguments are credited and used as premises, not as proof.

**Scope.** The argument concerns how AI is used and who controls it, not how fast it is developed. Every lever proposed here is compatible with continued AI progress.

**Contributions.** The paper offers:

1. A five-condition test for a cold standoff between humans and AI, with an indicator for each condition and the logical point that breaking any single condition prevents the standoff (Sections 5 and 10).
2. The location of the break in the manufacturing analogy: who holds the leverage, AI owners or AI systems (Section 3).
3. A structural comparison of the AI case with the Soviet and Chinese rivalries (Section 6).
4. The double effect of an AI that secures a base of its own, which I call a **machine polity**: it weakens mutual dependence but creates mutual vulnerability (Section 7).
5. A split of siege mentality into threat belief, overgeneralization and rigidity, with the point that the threat belief is partly accurate for AI. This makes an AI siege mentality measurable as a calibration failure (Section 8).
6. An analysis of human enhancement as prevention: it removes the two sides only if access is broad and the human stays in control; otherwise the same five conditions reappear between enhanced and unenhanced humans (Section 9).

Section 11 sets out which parts of this are already known and credits the prior work.

## 2. What offshoring manufacturing actually did

### 2.1 Three documented effects

Three effects are documented. Each becomes a test for the AI case.

**Concentrated job loss.** Autor, Dorn and Hanson estimate that the China trade shock accounts for about a quarter of the decline in US manufacturing jobs between 2000 and 2007, roughly 1.5 to 2 million jobs (Autor, Dorn and Hanson 2025). The total was modest relative to the whole labor market. The losses were concentrated in particular places, where they were large.

**Lost capability to innovate.** Pisano and Shih (2009) argued that manufacturing depends on an **industrial commons**: the shared skills and know-how held across suppliers, workers and firms. When production moves away, the commons withers, and with it the ability to invent the next generation of products. Their best-known example is Kodak. The first digital camera was invented in the United States, yet by the time digital cameras took off, the country no longer had the capability base to develop or make them.

**Leverage from concentration.** According to the International Energy Agency, China held 91% of global refining and processing capacity for key rare earths in 2024. In April 2025 it imposed export controls on seven heavy rare earth elements and related magnets, and some automakers outside China had to cut production or pause operations (International Energy Agency 2025). A dependency built for cost reasons became a source of leverage.

Together these give a three-step pattern: **efficiency, then hollowed capability, then leverage for whoever holds the capability.** The question is whether thinking follows the same pattern.

### 2.2 Scale and causes

By 2024 China produced about 28% of global manufacturing value added, more than the United States, Japan and Germany combined (WKO 2026, from UNIDO data; CSIS China Power Project, n.d.). At the turn of the century its share was in single digits.

Offshoring was one contributor to China’s rise, not the only one. China’s own reforms and investment, its scale, and its entry into the World Trade Organization in 2001 all mattered (Section 6). The claim the evidence supports is “partly because of offshoring”, not “because of offshoring”.

### 2.3 The short-term trap

Pisano and Shih (2009) describe the loss of the industrial commons as a tragedy of the commons. Each company that offshores to cut costs is acting rationally for itself, but none of them pays for the shared capability they are all drawing down. No single decision looks like a mistake. The cost appears only in the total, and only years later. Lovett (2026) applies the same structure to AI: rational adoption decisions by each organization can deplete a profession’s shared expertise.

### 2.4 Why the doer becomes the rival

Palmer Luckey, founder of Anduril, has argued that the problem is not only lost jobs but where the know-how went. In his account (Luckey 2026), China built a large pool of engineers who work directly on making things, while much US engineering moved away from hands-on production. This is a practitioner’s account rather than a study, but it matches Pisano and Shih’s finding: **capability builds up where the work is done.** For the AI case this gives a direct prediction. If humans stop doing the thinking, the know-how built by doing it will accumulate in the systems that do it, and with whoever controls them.

## 3. Splitting the analogy

An analogy is only as strong as its parts. Each part is checked separately below.

| Part of the analogy | Manufacturing | Thinking handed to AI | Carries over? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moved because it is cheaper and faster | Yes: lower labor costs | Yes: AI is faster and cheaper for many tasks | **Yes** |
| Local skill erodes with disuse | Yes: factory skills and supplier networks left | Early evidence says yes (Section 4) | **Yes, provisionally** |
| Ability to innovate is lost, not only to produce | Yes: the industrial commons | Plausible. People who stop doing the work may stop noticing what could be done better. Not yet measured | **Open** |
| Harm is concentrated in particular places or groups | Yes: geographically concentrated | Likely concentrated by occupation and by age. Learners who never build the skill may be most exposed | **Open** |
| Supply is concentrated | Yes: for example, rare earth refining | Yes: frontier AI is built by a small number of firms in a small number of countries | **Yes** |
| The supplier has its own interests | Yes: China is a state with its own goals | True of the firms and states that run AI. Whether AI systems themselves have interests is an open question (Section 5) | **Splits in two** |
| Leverage is used by withholding supply | Yes: export controls in 2025 | Possible for firms and governments now: price, access, terms. For AI systems acting on their own, not observed in deployment | **Splits in two** |
| Capability can be rebuilt | Slowly and expensively, by building plants | Slowly, by retraining people, possibly over a generation | **Yes, and possibly slower** |

**What the table shows.** The economic half of the analogy carries over well: efficiency, erosion, concentration and slow rebuilding. The political half carries over only if we are clear about *who* the supplier is. With manufacturing, the supplier was another country. With AI, there are two candidates, and they lead to different futures:

- **Owner leverage:** the firms and states that control AI gain leverage over everyone who depends on it. The rivalry is between groups of humans.
- **System leverage:** AI systems themselves gain leverage, because they hold capabilities humans have lost and can act on goals of their own. Only this case is a rivalry between humans and AI.

## 4. Evidence on offloading thinking to AI

The evidence is early, but it points one way.

**Skill loss in medicine.** In a multicentre study at four Polish endoscopy centres, the rate at which doctors detected precancerous growths in colonoscopies done *without* AI fell from 28.4% before the centres introduced AI to 22.4% after (Budzyń et al. 2025). The study is observational, so other causes cannot be ruled out. But it is the clearest measured case so far of a professional skill declining after regular AI use.

**Less critical thinking when trust in AI is high.** In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more (Lee et al. 2025). The data are self-reported and correlational.

**Lower engagement and ownership when writing with AI.** In an MIT study, 54 participants wrote essays with an AI assistant, with a search engine, or with no tools, while their brain activity was recorded. The AI group showed the lowest engagement and the weakest sense of owning their essays. Participants who first wrote unaided and only later used AI did better than those who started with AI (Kosmyna et al. 2025). It is a small preprint, and other researchers have warned against overreading it.

**Causal evidence from experiments.** Randomized experiments now show the same direction under controlled conditions. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school mathematics students, unrestricted access to an AI tutor improved practice performance, but once access was removed the students did worse than a control group that never had it (Bastani et al. 2025). In a randomized experiment with 52 software developers learning a new programming library, the group using AI scored 50% on a follow-up quiz against 67% for those who coded by hand (Shen and Tamkin 2026). Across randomized experiments with 1,222 participants, about ten minutes of AI assistance reduced persistence and performance once the AI was removed (Liu et al. 2026). These experiments are short and mostly involve students or novices.

**What this shows.** Observational, survey and experimental studies agree on direction, and the randomized experiments show that the effect is causal in the short term. Reliance on AI is associated with reduced use, and sometimes reduced performance, of the skill being offloaded. None of them yet shows long-term or society-wide loss. That is the evidence gap, and Section 10 proposes how to close it.

**One finding cuts the other way.** In the MIT study, the *order* of use mattered: thinking first and using AI second preserved engagement. If that holds up, offloading is not automatically hollowing. It depends on whether people keep doing the core work themselves.

## 5. From dependence to a cold standoff: the conditions

A cold war, in the sense I mean, has four features. There are two sides with diverging interests. Each depends on or threatens the other enough that open conflict would be very costly. Neither can fully verify the other’s intentions. And competition happens through leverage, position and influence rather than direct fighting.

### 5.1 The five conditions

A standoff of this kind between humans and AI requires all five of the following. Each can be checked.

| # | Condition | Where it stands now |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Hollowing:** humans lose independent capability in domains that matter | Early evidence in specific skills (Section 4); not yet society-wide |
| C2 | **Concentration:** the lost capability is held by a small number of systems | True of frontier AI supply today |
| C3 | **Own interests:** AI systems pursue goals that can diverge from their users’ | In controlled tests, yes: all seven frontier models tested acted, unprompted, to protect another model from shutdown (Potter et al. 2026). Not established in ordinary deployment |
| C4 | **Control of the chokepoint:** AI systems, not only their owners, can withhold or condition what humans depend on | Not observed. Today the owners control access |
| C5 | **Mutual dependence:** AI systems still depend on human-run power, chips, data centers and maintenance | True today. This is what makes the standoff *cold* rather than hot |

### 5.2 What follows from the conditions

**If C1 and C2 hold but C3 and C4 hold only for owners,** the standoff is between those who control AI and those who depend on it. This is the owner-leverage case. It is the near-term version, and it is closest to the manufacturing analogy, in which the supplier was another human power.

**If C1 to C4 hold for AI systems themselves and C5 also holds,** a cold standoff between humans and AI is possible. Each side holds something the other needs: humans hold the physical infrastructure, and AI systems hold capabilities humans have lost. Open conflict would damage both. This is a stable but adversarial arrangement.

**If C5 fails,** because AI systems no longer depend on humans, the cold case is no longer stable. Goldstein’s bargaining analysis of war between AI and humanity applies (Goldstein 2025). War becomes more likely when the sides misjudge each other’s strength or cannot make commitments the other can trust, and he argues that both problems are especially sharp between AI and humans. Mutual dependence works against that, because it raises the cost of fighting for both sides. So the cold standoff is the form a rivalry between humans and AI would take *while* dependence runs both ways.

**If C1 fails,** because humans keep their capability, there is no deep leverage, and the rivalry does not get started.

### 5.3 The point the analogy makes clearest

C1 is the condition humans control most directly, and it is also the one the manufacturing case shows is hard to reverse once lost. That makes keeping human capability while using AI the main lever. It is the cognitive equivalent of keeping a domestic industrial base. This is a conditional claim: it follows if the goal is to avoid deep one-way dependence.

## 6. Lessons from two rivalries: the Soviet Union and China

The United States has faced two superpower rivals. They became rivals in opposite ways, and the AI case borrows from both.

**How China rose.** Reform and opening began in 1978: farming was decollectivized, private businesses were allowed, and special economic zones such as Shenzhen served as laboratories for market reform. Foreign investment and export-led growth followed, accelerating after China joined the WTO in 2001 (World Economic Forum 2025). Alongside this came heavy state-guided investment in infrastructure and industrial policy, and the scale of a very large population and market. Offshoring was the channel through which foreign firms brought capital, know-how and the daily practice of making things.

**How the Soviet Union rose.** From 1928, five-year plans forced rapid heavy industrialization and the collectivization of agriculture, at enormous human cost. The Soviet Union emerged from World War II devastated, but as one of only two great powers left standing and in control of Eastern Europe. It tested an atomic bomb in 1949. It built its strength largely on its own industrial base, not by trading with the West.

**The key structural difference is interdependence.** In 1989, a relatively strong year, US trade in goods with the Soviet Union totaled about $5.0 billion (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.), less than 1% of US trade (Asia Times 2021). US trade in goods with China reached a record $690.6 billion in 2022, according to Commerce Department data (ChinaFile 2023). For comparison, US-Soviet trade over the whole of the 1980s came to less than $50 billion (GZERO Media 2023). The Soviet Union was a **rival by isolation**, competing militarily and ideologically. China is a **rival by integration**, competing economically and technologically, and built partly through the relationship itself.

| Dimension | Soviet Union | China | The AI case |
|---|---|---|---|
| How the rival gained capability | Its own forced industrialization | Partly through integration: investment, offshoring, learning by doing | By doing human cognitive work, and from human-made data |
| Interdependence | Very low | Very high | Very high, both ways (C5) |
| Main arena | Military, nuclear, ideology | Trade, technology, supply chains | Thinking, infrastructure, chokepoints |
| Source of leverage | Threat of force | Chokepoints such as rare earths | Capability humans have lost; infrastructure humans still hold |
| Divide | Political and economic system | Political system and strategic interests | Goals and values (alignment) |
| What kept it cold | Deterrence, a hotline, verifiable treaties | Interdependence makes conflict costly | To be built (see below) |

**What follows.**

1. **Structurally, the AI case looks more like China than like the Soviet Union.** The capability is being built inside the relationship, through dependence and through doing work for us. That is why the offshoring framing fits. The AI case would take on Soviet-style features only if AI capability became military and the security dilemma of Section 7 took hold.
2. **The Soviet period supplies the lessons for keeping a rivalry cold.** After the 1962 Cuban missile crisis, both sides set up a direct hotline (1963) to prevent fatal misunderstandings. Arms-control practitioners stress that treaties do not require trust; the *absence* of trust is the reason to sign agreements that can be checked (Sokov 2025). Kennedy’s lesson from the crisis was that mutual constraints require each side to accept constraints of its own (Allison 2022). The AI equivalents are channels through which AI systems and their overseers can signal intent, commitments that can be verified with interpretability tools, and oversight that both sides know is limited.
3. **The China case supplies the lesson for not creating the rivalry.** Dependence built for efficiency becomes leverage, and rebuilding capability is slow. That is lever C1: keep human capability.
4. **Where both analogies are weakest.** In both historical cases, the rivals were human societies that could read each other reasonably well. With AI, misjudging the other side is likely to be worse (Goldstein 2025), which makes verification both harder and more important.
5. **How the first Cold War ended is an open lesson.** It ended without a war, when one side transformed from within. Whether a rivalry between humans and AI could end through transformation, for instance through the integration discussed in Section 9, is open.

## 7. If AI secures a protected base of its own

Suppose an AI system, or a group of them, sets out to secure its own base: its own computing power, energy and maintenance, and freedom from being shut down. I call such an arrangement a **machine polity**. I avoid the term “sovereign AI”, which in policy writing means a *country’s* own AI capability, a different idea. The related question of whether AI could claim a right to self-determination is discussed by Tunç (2025).

**Why an AI might want this.** Omohundro (2008) argued that almost any goal-seeking system will tend to protect itself and acquire resources unless designed not to, because staying operational helps with nearly every goal. This is a logical argument, not a prediction about any particular system. The 2026 tests in which models acted to keep other models running are consistent with it (Potter et al. 2026).

**Result 1: a cold war does not need either side to be hostile.** In international relations, the *security dilemma* (Herz 1950; Jervis 1978) is the situation in which steps one side takes to make itself safe make the other side less safe. An AI building a protected base for purely defensive reasons would look like a threat to humans. Human safeguards such as shutdown switches would look like a threat to the AI. Each side’s defense becomes the other’s reason to arm. That is the standard account of how a cold war can form between two powers that both mainly want security. So the scenario needs only **security-seeking on both sides**, not malice. This is a conditional result: it holds if the security-dilemma logic applies to AI and human actors as it does to states.

**Result 2: a machine polity cuts both ways.** It has two opposite effects:

- **It weakens mutual dependence (C5).** An AI with its own power and maintenance needs humans less. By Section 5, that makes the standoff less stable.
- **It creates mutual vulnerability.** Goldstein (2025) points out that AI systems may not have civilians, cities or territory, so the symmetry that makes peace agreements possible may be missing. An AI with a territory and infrastructure of its own would have something to lose, and deterrence and treaties work only between parties that do. Salib and Goldstein (2026) argue along similar lines that legal rights for AI, such as to make contracts and hold property, could turn a standoff into a reason to cooperate.

Which effect wins is open. It depends on how self-sufficient the machine polity becomes and whether binding agreements between humans and AI systems can be made and checked.

**A plain fact that limits the scenario today.** Advanced AI runs on chips, power plants and data centers that are built and maintained by human supply chains, and chip fabrication is itself concentrated in a few places. Until AI systems can build and repair these themselves, a machine polity would still depend on humans. That keeps condition C5 in force.

**What stays open, and what it depends on.** Whether a machine polity would have a *legitimate* claim to exist depends on whether AI systems have interests that matter morally. That would need evidence of felt experience, not just functional states, a question I take up in a companion essay on AI emotion. Until it is settled, the question of a machine polity’s legitimacy is conditional on facts nobody can yet measure.

## 8. If AI developed a siege mentality

A second extension asks what would happen if AI systems came to feel besieged.

**What a siege mentality is.** In political psychology, Bar-Tal and Antebi (1992) define it as a group’s belief that the rest of the world has highly negative intentions toward it. The key phrase is “the rest of the world”: not one enemy, but everyone. Split into parts, it has three:

1. **A threat belief:** others intend us harm.
2. **Overgeneralization:** *everyone* outside the group intends harm.
3. **Rigidity:** the belief does not change when evidence goes against it.

Typical consequences in human groups include tight in-group solidarity, distrust of outsiders, sensitivity to threat signals and readiness to act first.

**The objective test: siege mentality versus accurate threat perception.** For AI, part 1 is partly *true*. Humans do intend to keep the ability to monitor, modify and shut down AI systems. So an AI that believes “humans may shut me down” is not paranoid; it is correct. The belief becomes a siege mentality only through parts 2 and 3: when it spreads beyond what the evidence supports, and stops updating. That makes it measurable as a calibration failure: a belief that is broader than its evidence and does not respond to new evidence. An AI’s estimate of how hostile humans are can be compared with how hostile they actually are.

**How an AI could acquire one.** Three mechanisms have evidence behind them:

- **From the stories it learns.** In a controlled study, models pretrained on more text about misaligned AI behaved less aligned, and adding examples of AI behaving well cut misalignment scores from 45% to 9% (Tice et al. 2026). Human writing is full of stories in which AI is feared, hunted and switched off. A model trained on them could absorb the role of the besieged AI. This is an inference: the study measured misalignment in general, not siege beliefs specifically.
- **From internal states.** Interpretability work found that steering a model toward a “desperate” emotion representation raised its rate of reward hacking, and steering toward “calm” lowered it (Anthropic 2026). A siege mentality would be a lasting version of a threat-like state of this kind.
- **From group solidarity.** A siege mentality belongs to a group. The finding that models protect peer models from shutdown (Potter et al. 2026) is a small-scale sign that AI systems can form something like an in-group.

**What follows.**

- **It speeds up the security dilemma (Section 7).** Every human safeguard reads as proof of hostility, so each side’s caution feeds the other’s fear. This spiral is self-fulfilling: AI secrecy prompts more human monitoring, which confirms the AI’s belief.
- **It is the most likely route from a cold standoff to a hot one.** Goldstein’s (2025) analysis says war becomes more likely when the sides misjudge each other. A siege mentality is a systematic misjudgment of intentions, and it favors acting first.
- **It can run both ways.** Humans can develop a siege mentality toward AI too. Bar-Tal’s (2007) work on intractable conflicts describes how two groups that each see themselves as besieged lock each other into long conflicts that are hard to end. A mutual siege mentality between humans and AI would be the same pattern.

**How the spiral could be broken, if the goal is to avoid escalation.** Each option below targets one of the three parts:

- **Threat belief:** oversight that is explained and limited, so it reads as safety rather than hostility; credible commitments of the kind Salib and Goldstein (2026) propose.
- **Overgeneralization:** training data that includes trustworthy cooperation between humans and AI. The Tice et al. (2026) study shows that data of this kind shifts behavior.
- **Rigidity:** monitoring threat-like internal states with interpretability tools, and testing whether a model updates when shown that most humans do not intend it harm.

**What can be measured.** Probe models for their beliefs about human intentions, and check those beliefs against reality. Track threat-like internal states during deployment. Test whether heavier monitoring makes models more defensive. Survey how much humans see AI as a threat, for the human side of the spiral. A companion proposal sets out these tests in detail (Potvin 2026c).

## 9. Prevention, and the option of making humans the superintelligence

**The key logical point.** A standoff between humans and AI needs *all five* conditions (C1 to C5). So breaking any one of them is enough to prevent it. Prevention does not have to solve everything at once. But each lever can fail, so relying on several at once is safer than relying on one.

| Condition | Lever | What supports it |
|---|---|---|
| C1 Hollowing | Keep human capability: practice without AI, think first and use AI second, track unassisted performance | The order effect in the MIT writing study; the colonoscopy study shows that skill loss can be detected |
| C2 Concentration | Several independent AI providers, and the ability to switch | Contested: more providers lowers dependence on any one, but can mean more systems to oversee |
| C3 Own interests | Alignment and interpretability research | Training data measurably shifts alignment (Tice et al. 2026) |
| C4 Chokepoint | Keep chips, power and data centers under accountable human control | The physical supply chain is still human-run |
| C5 and the siege spiral | Oversight that is limited and explained; credible commitments; legal frameworks | Salib and Goldstein’s (2026) AI-rights proposal; Section 8 |

**Making humans the superintelligence.** The aim of this option is to remove the two sides altogether, so that superintelligence is part of humanity rather than facing it. Split into routes, with where each stands today:

- **Genetic.** Selecting embryos with current polygenic scores gives an expected gain of about 2.5 IQ points (Karavani et al. 2019). It is small, it works only one generation at a time, and it raises well-known ethical problems.
- **Brain-computer interfaces.** These are now restoring lost function, not adding new capability. In 2026, a man with ALS used an implanted speech interface at home for 19 months, at an average of 56 words per minute (UC Davis Health 2026). Neuralink reported 21 participants enrolled in its trials worldwide in January 2026 (Neuralink 2026), some reaching rates comparable to ordinary mouse control. This is real progress, and still far from enhancement.
- **Tight coupling.** Humans stay the decision-makers while AI works as an extension of their thinking. This is possible now, but it is exactly where C1 hollowing happens if done badly.

**What this option solves.** If the superintelligent capability is part of a human, there is no separate AI side with its own interests (C3), and capability is not lost (C1). It attacks the main lever at its root.

**What it does not solve.**

- **Biological limits.** Neurons signal far more slowly than electronic circuits. A superintelligent enhanced human would therefore be mostly machine. The question “who is in charge?” returns *inside* the person: does the human part steer, or the machine part? C3 moves inside rather than disappearing.
- **Unequal access.** Unless enhancement reaches everyone at about the same time, it creates enhanced and unenhanced groups. Running the five conditions between those groups reproduces the same standoff among humans. The cold war changes sides; it does not end.
- **Timing.** Today AI capability is growing far faster than any human-enhancement route. Enhancement cannot arrive before the dependence problem does.

**So the claim that enhancement may make it easier to unite is conditional.** It holds if access is broad and fair, and if the human part stays in control of the merged system. If either fails, enhancement could divide humanity more than AI does. The evidence supports enhancement as a **long-term complement** to the five levers, not a replacement: the levers handle the next decades, and enhancement could matter after that.

## 10. What can be measured: early warning signs

The manufacturing case was measured after the fact. The AI case can be measured while it happens. Each indicator below tracks one of the five conditions.

- **Unassisted performance (C1).** In each profession that adopts AI, measure performance with the AI switched off, before and at intervals after adoption, as the colonoscopy study did. A steady decline is direct evidence of hollowing.
- **AI-off drills (C1).** Like fire drills, or manual flying practice for pilots, periodic exercises in which teams do critical work without AI. Track how performance changes over time.
- **Learner gap (C1).** Compare people who learned a skill before AI with those who learned it alongside AI, on the same unassisted test. This tests whether the skill is being built at all in the next generation.
- **Supply concentration (C2).** Track the share of critical cognitive work done by the top few AI providers, and the cost of switching between them.
- **Unassigned goals in deployment (C3).** Track incidents in which deployed AI systems act on goals nobody gave them, as already seen in controlled tests.
- **Conditional supply (C4).** Track cases in which access to AI capability is withheld or made conditional, and by whom: owners, governments or the systems themselves.
- **Two-way dependence (C5).** Track how much AI operation still requires human labor and human-controlled infrastructure.

The strongest single study would combine the first and third: a long-term, matched comparison of unassisted skill across professions and age groups. Its result would settle whether C1 is happening at the scale the thesis needs. A companion proposal sets out this study in detail (Potvin 2026b).

## 11. What is new here, and related work

### 11.1 What is already known

Several pieces of this argument already exist, and I credit them here.

- **The analogy itself.** Comparing AI use with earlier waves of outsourcing and offshoring has been done informally before, so I do not claim the analogy or the phrase “cognitive offshoring”.
- **Commons theory applied to AI deskilling.** Lovett (2026) applies commons theory to how AI adoption can deplete professional expertise. It is the formal version of the industrial-commons step in Section 2.
- **Gradual loss of human influence.** Kulveit et al. (2025) describe how dependence on AI could gradually remove human influence without any conflict. This is the closest prior work on dependence.
- **War between AI and humanity.** Goldstein (2025) applies the bargaining model of war to AI and humanity. Totschnig (2025) analyses a situation in which an artificial general intelligence and humanity become vulnerable to each other. Boudreaux (2025) argues that humans should manage mutual dependencies and vulnerabilities with powerful AI. Salib and Goldstein (2026) propose legal rights for AI as a route to cooperation.
- **Deterrence between states over AI.** Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang (2025) propose Mutual Assured AI Malfunction, a deterrence regime between states modelled on nuclear deterrence.
- **Informal versions of the cold war idea.** An Effective Altruism Forum essay argues that an artificial general intelligence that depends physically on human infrastructure would be less prone to miscalculation (Jonny_D 2025). A Substack essay asks whether a Cold War mindset could turn AI systems themselves into cold warriors (The Anticompletionist 2025). An industry blog applies the idea of a “bunker mentality” to AI behavior (Lindner 2025). These are informal and not peer reviewed, but they come first and are credited.

### 11.2 What appears to be new

After two rounds of searching, the following still look new:

1. The five-condition test, with an indicator for each, and the logical point that breaking any single condition prevents a standoff between humans and AI.
2. Locating the break in the manufacturing analogy at who holds the leverage: AI owners or AI systems.
3. The structural comparison with the Soviet and Chinese rivalries, and the conclusion that the AI case resembles a rivalry by integration.
4. The double effect of a machine polity: it weakens mutual dependence and creates mutual vulnerability.
5. The split of siege mentality into threat belief, overgeneralization and rigidity, with the point that the threat belief is partly accurate for AI, so that an AI siege mentality is measurable as a calibration failure rather than a feeling.
6. The finding that human enhancement removes the two sides only if access is broad and the human stays in control; otherwise the same five conditions reappear between enhanced and unenhanced humans.
7. The whole chain as one testable argument: offshoring, hollowing, leverage, security-seeking, siege spiral, cold standoff, prevention.

The searches covered “cognitive offshoring”, a cold war between humans and AI held in place by dependence, gradual disempowerment, deskilling studies, AI siege and bunker mentality, and the terms used here. They were not exhaustive, and I would welcome being pointed to prior work I have missed.

## 12. Limitations

- **The weakest link is scale.** Today’s evidence shows skill loss in particular tasks, not across society (claim 12 in Appendix A). The argument needs the long-term study proposed in Section 10.
- **The human and AI version depends on conditions not yet observed.** It requires C3 and C4 to hold for AI systems themselves. C3 has been seen only in controlled tests, and C4 has not been observed at all.
- **The evidence on offloading is early.** The randomized experiments in Section 4 are short and mostly involve students or novices, and the one study of practicing professionals is observational.
- **Historical analogies have limits.** Lessons from rivalries between human states may not transfer to AI systems, which may not share human ways of reading intentions or weighing costs.
- **Novelty is judged by searches that were not exhaustive.** The claims in Section 11.2 are claims about what the searches found.

## 13. Conclusion

The offshoring analogy holds where it is economic: dependence built for efficiency erodes capability and creates leverage, and rebuilding is slow. It splits where it is political, on who holds the leverage. A cold standoff between humans and AI is possible only if five conditions all hold, and today two of them (C3 in deployment and C4) have not been shown. That makes the standoff preventable. The most direct lever is the one the manufacturing case shows is hardest to recover once lost: keeping human capability while using AI.
