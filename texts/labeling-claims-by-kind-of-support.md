## Abstract

Philosophical and policy writing mixes measured findings, logical inferences, distinctions, conditional claims, predictions and open questions, usually without telling the reader which is which. Readers then have no quick way to see which parts of an argument rest on evidence, which follow from premises, and which are still undecided. This note sets out a simple discipline, used throughout the Universal Inquiry research program: every main claim in a paper is listed in a table with its kind, its basis and its status, and no main claim may rest only on opinion. It defines six kinds of claim (Evidence, Logic, Distinction, Conditional, Prediction and Open), a set of status values, and six rules, including that other writers’ views enter only as credited premises and that every open or predictive claim must name what would decide it. A worked example is taken from a paper on AI dependence. The note relates the method to GRADE ratings of evidence, the IPCC’s calibrated uncertainty language, Toulmin’s model of argument and the informal “epistemic status” convention, and states what it adds.

**Keywords:** philosophical method; argumentation; claim labeling; evidence grading; uncertainty communication; transparency; research methods

## 1. The problem

A typical argument in philosophy or policy contains several kinds of claim at once. Some report what was measured. Some follow from premises. Some draw a distinction. Some hold only if a goal or condition is granted. Some predict, and some are simply undecided. Prose tends to give them all the same confident voice.

Two problems follow. Readers cannot quickly see where an argument is strong and where it is weak. And authority can leak into the argument: a well-known writer’s view can end up doing the work of evidence without anyone noticing. The method below makes each claim’s support visible, so that readers can check it and authors can be corrected on the right grounds.

## 2. Six kinds of claim

Each main claim is given one kind, chosen by what actually supports it. Each kind carries an obligation: something the author must supply for the claim to stand.

| Kind | What supports it | What the author must supply |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Measured data or a checkable plain fact | The source, and the limits of the study design (for example observational, self-reported or small) |
| **Logic** | Valid reasoning from stated premises | The premises; the claim holds only if they hold |
| **Distinction** | Two things often treated as one can come apart, or two things treated as different are the same | At least one case in which they come apart, or the shared feature |
| **Conditional** | Holds only if a stated condition or goal is granted | The condition, written into the claim itself |
| **Prediction** | A claim about what will happen | What would confirm or refute it, and when, if possible |
| **Open** | Cannot be decided on current evidence | What would decide it |

## 3. Status

Each claim also carries a status, recording where it stands now:

- **Checked:** an Evidence claim whose source has been verified.
- **Holds:** a Logic or Distinction claim whose reasoning has been checked on its stated premises.
- **Conditional:** holds only under the stated condition.
- **Not yet shown** or **not yet tested:** an Open or Prediction claim still waiting for its decider.
- **Untested:** a claim that could be tested but has not been.

A short note can follow the status, such as “observational, one setting” or “tests, not deployment”.

## 4. Six rules

1. **No main claim rests only on opinion.** A claim with no evidence, no logic and no stated condition is either removed or labeled Open with its decider.
2. **Other writers’ views are premises, not proof.** They are credited by name and used as premises, or listed in related work. They never count as evidence merely because of who holds them.
3. **Several causes are marked.** When a cause is one of several, the claim says “partly”.
4. **Every Open or Prediction claim names its decider.** This is what makes the table a list of tests rather than a list of assertions.
5. **Novelty is labeled by what searches found.** Claims of novelty use fixed labels (known; new combination; no prior version found; white space), name the searches run, and say that the searches were not exhaustive.
6. **Corrections follow the kind.** An Evidence claim is corrected by better evidence, and a Logic claim by finding a flaw in the reasoning or a false premise. When a claim is corrected, the table is updated and the change is recorded.

## 5. A worked example

The following rows are taken from a paper on dependence on AI (Potvin 2026, Appendix A).

| Claim | Kind | Basis | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentrated supply of rare earths became leverage | Evidence | IEA data; 2025 export controls | Checked |
| A cold standoff between humans and AI requires all five stated conditions | Logic | Each condition supplies one feature of a cold standoff | Holds |
| Keeping human capability is the main lever | Conditional | If the goal is to avoid deep one-way dependence | Holds given the goal |
| Hollowing is happening at society-wide scale | Open | Needs a long-term study of unassisted skill | Not yet shown |

The table shows at a glance that the paper’s strongest claims are its evidence and its logic, that its main recommendation depends on a stated goal, and that its largest empirical question is still open, together with the study that would settle it.

## 6. Related work, and what is new

**Credited prior work.**

- **GRADE** rates the quality of a body of evidence and the strength of recommendations in medicine, using an explicit and transparent system (Guyatt et al. 2008).
- **The IPCC’s guidance on uncertainty** gives authors a common, calibrated language for confidence in findings and for the likelihood of outcomes (Mastrandrea et al. 2010).
- **Toulmin’s model of argument** separates a claim from its grounds, the warrant connecting them, and the qualifier limiting it (Toulmin 1958).
- **The “epistemic status” convention**, common on online forums such as LessWrong and the Effective Altruism Forum, asks authors to state at the top of a post how confident they are and how much effort went into it (see Raemon 2018).

**What appears to be new.**

1. Labeling each claim by the **kind** of support it has, not only by how strong or likely it is. GRADE and the IPCC grade strength and confidence; this method first sorts claims by what kind of thing could support or overturn them.
2. The rule that other writers’ views count only as credited premises, which keeps authority from standing in for evidence.
3. The requirement that every open or predictive claim name its decider, which turns the table into a list of tests.
4. Fixed labels for novelty claims, tied to the searches that support them.
5. Applying all of this to philosophical writing, where claims are rarely labeled at all.

These are claims about what my searches found. The searches covered claim labeling, evidence grading, argument annotation and the epistemic status convention, and they were not exhaustive.

## 7. Limitations

- **Classification can be contested.** A claim may have both evidence and logic behind it. In that case the author records the support that the claim actually depends on, and readers can dispute the label.
- **Labels are only as good as the checking behind them.** A table can make a weak argument look orderly. The status column exists to show how much checking has been done.
- **The method does not replace review.** It makes review easier by showing reviewers where to look.
- **Kinds are not a ranking.** An Evidence claim from one small study can be weaker than a Logic claim with secure premises.
